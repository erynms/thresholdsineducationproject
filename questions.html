Open Questions

While this special issue advances our understanding of generative AI in education significantly, it also reveals tensions, limitations, and questions that remain unresolved. These open questions point toward future research, ongoing dialogue, and continued critical engagement.

Tensions, Limitations & What Remains Unresolved

The Speed Problem

One of the most significant challenges acknowledged across the issue is that AI development is outpacing research and pedagogy:

  • Articles written about ChatGPT-3.5 may already be outdated by the time of publication
  • New capabilities emerge faster than educators can develop appropriate pedagogical responses
  • Research findings may become obsolete before they can inform practice
  • Students may be using newer tools than faculty are aware of

This creates a perpetual challenge: how do we develop thoughtful, evidence-based approaches to technologies that are constantly evolving?

The Support Gap

Throughout the issue, a consistent frustration emerges: faculty are left without adequate training or institutional guidance:

  • Individual instructors must navigate complex pedagogical questions alone
  • Institutional policies lag behind technological change (or are absent entirely)
  • Professional development opportunities remain limited
  • Faculty workload increases without corresponding support or compensation
  • Inconsistent policies across departments create confusion for students

The question remains: what institutional structures and support systems would enable faculty to engage thoughtfully with AI rather than reactively scrambling to respond?

The "Black Box" Problem

Several articles note that even developers don't fully understand how LLMs work:

  • Emergent behaviors that weren't programmed or predicted
  • Inability to fully explain why models generate specific outputs
  • Hidden biases that reflect training data in unpredictable ways
  • Lack of transparency from companies about training data and methods

This opacity creates challenges for educators trying to teach students to engage critically with AI. How can we teach critical engagement with technologies whose inner workings remain mysterious even to their creators?

Persistent Questions About Learning

The issue identifies—but cannot definitively answer—fundamental questions about how students learn:

How do students learn with vs. without AI?

  • Does revision of AI-generated text develop the same skills as drafting from scratch?
  • What cognitive processes are engaged or bypassed when using AI?
  • Can students develop critical thinking by critiquing AI without using it themselves?
  • What is the long-term impact on learning when students regularly use AI tools?

Can we build AI literacy while maintaining critical skepticism?

  • How do we teach students to use AI effectively without normalizing problematic aspects?
  • Is it possible to be both competent with AI and appropriately skeptical of it?
  • What does "literacy" mean when the technology itself is rapidly changing?

What does "authentic" writing mean now?

  • If AI can generate competent text, what makes human writing valuable?
  • How do we define and assess "authenticity" in an age of AI collaboration?
  • Should we even maintain "authenticity" as a value, or is it outdated?
  • What kinds of writing assignments remain meaningful when AI can complete traditional tasks?

Issues Largely Unaddressed in This Issue

While the special issue makes significant contributions, several important concerns receive limited attention:

Ecological Costs

  • Energy consumption and carbon footprint of training and running LLMs
  • Environmental impact of widespread AI adoption in education
  • Ethical implications of using environmentally costly tools for educational purposes

Bias Amplification

  • How AI reproduces and potentially amplifies existing biases in language and culture
  • Impacts on multilingual students and non-standard language varieties
  • Representation (or lack thereof) of diverse perspectives in AI-generated content
  • Perpetuation of dominant cultural narratives through AI training data

These gaps point toward areas where future research and critical engagement are urgently needed.

Higher Ed at the Crossroads: Moving Forward Thoughtfully

Faculty Positioned to Lead Critical Engagement

Despite the challenges, the issue affirms that faculty are uniquely positioned to lead thoughtful engagement with AI:

  • Expertise in pedagogy and student learning
  • Commitment to critical thinking and ethical reasoning
  • Direct relationships with students navigating these technologies
  • Ability to study AI implementation empirically in classroom contexts
  • Professional autonomy to experiment with approaches

However, this potential can only be realized with adequate support and resources.

The Need for Institutional Support

Moving forward thoughtfully requires institutions to provide:

  • Professional development: Ongoing opportunities to learn about AI technologies and pedagogical approaches
  • Clear, stable policies: Institutional guidelines that provide direction without being overly prescriptive
  • Time and resources: Recognition that adapting to AI requires significant faculty labor
  • Support for experimentation: Permission to try approaches and learn from failures
  • Collaborative spaces: Opportunities for faculty to share experiences and develop collective approaches

A Both/And Approach

The path forward suggested by this issue is one that acknowledges risks while exploring possibilities:

  • Both skeptical of corporate AI hype and open to pedagogical potential
  • Both concerned about student learning and trusting of student capability
  • Both committed to traditional writing values and willing to reconsider assumptions
  • Both protective of faculty autonomy and collaborative in developing approaches

This both/and stance resists simple narratives of either resistance or adoption, instead embracing the complexity of the moment.

Central Commitments

Regardless of how individual educators approach AI, the issue suggests several non-negotiable commitments:

  • Maintaining human agency: Students remain decision-makers and critical thinkers
  • Preserving creativity: Education values original thought and expression
  • Fostering critical thinking: Students learn to analyze, evaluate, and question
  • Ensuring equity: AI tools don't exacerbate existing educational inequalities
  • Protecting privacy: Student data and intellectual property are safeguarded

These commitments should guide pedagogical decisions about AI, serving as touchstones when navigating uncertain terrain.

Evidence-Based Optimism Grounded in Classroom Realities

This special issue offers a form of cautious optimism—not naive enthusiasm, but hope grounded in evidence:

  • Students can make thoughtful choices about AI use
  • Faculty can develop pedagogical approaches that maintain educational values
  • Critical engagement can replace surveillance and prohibition
  • Writing instruction can adapt without abandoning its core purposes

This optimism is tempered by awareness of challenges, uncertainties, and unresolved questions. But it suggests that thoughtful engagement is both possible and preferable to either uncritical adoption or defensive refusal.

Concluding Thoughts

The questions raised in this special issue are ultimately more valuable than any definitive answers could be. They invite ongoing conversation, continued experimentation, and critical reflection. As AI technologies continue to evolve, these questions will shift and new ones will emerge. The commitment to asking them thoughtfully—to treating generative AI as an object of critical inquiry rather than simply a tool or threat—may be this issue's most important contribution.
