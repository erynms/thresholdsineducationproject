Analysis

New Rhetorical Understandings

This special issue of Thresholds makes several significant contributions to ongoing conversations about generative AI in education (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, p. 5). These contributions span conceptual, methodological, and practical domain.

1. Conceptual Shifts

The articles collectively reconceptualize fundamental terms in writing studies, opening up potential for reimagining our pedagogy:

  • Voice: No longer understood as purely individual expression, but as potentially collaborative and distributed across human-AI partnerships (Krueger, 2025) (Ryan et al., 2025, p. 48-49)
  • Agency: Reimagined as "co-agency" where students maintain authority while engaging AI tools (Krueger, 2025) (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, p. 26) (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, p. 5)
  • Authorship: Boundaries challenged and renegotiated, opening questions about what counts as "original" work (Ryan et al., 2025, p. 40) (Ryan et al., 2025, p. 42)
  • Collaboration: Extended beyond human-to-human interaction to include human-AI collaboration (Nelson et al., 2025) (Li, 2025, p. 9)

These conceptual shifts don't abandon traditional values in writing instruction (Krueger, 2025); rather, they complicate our understanding of these terms in light of new technological realities.

2. Methodological Contributions

The issue demonstrates the value of empirical classroom studies over speculation (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, p. 5):

  • Multiple articles report on actual classroom implementations and student responses (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, p. 25) (Li, 2025, p. 8) (Ryan et al., 2025, p. 41-42)
  • Evidence-based findings replace anxious predictions or enthusiastic promises (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, p. 3)
  • Grounded theory methods reveal patterns in AI-generated feedback (Velez et al., 2025)
  • Student surveys and reflections provide insight into learner perspectives (Li, 2025, p. 13-15) (Ryan et al., 2025, p. 42)

This methodological grounding makes the issue particularly valuable for practitioners seeking guidance based on actual practice rather than theoretical speculation.

3. Pedagogical Frameworks

The articles provide practical approaches adaptable to varied teaching contexts:

  • Frameworks for collaborative intelligence between humans and AI (Nelson et al., 2025)
  • Assignment designs that integrate AI while maintaining pedagogical integrity (Krueger, 2025) (Li, 2025, p. 10-11)
  • Assessment approaches that evaluate both products and processes (Uanachain & Aouad, 2025)
  • Guidelines for transparent AI policies in the classroom (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, p. 31) (Nelson et al., 2025)
  • Methods for building algorithmic literacy through critical engagement (Nelson et al., 2025)

These frameworks offer flexible starting points that educators can adapt to their own institutional contexts and pedagogical values (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, p. 3).

4. Critical Perspectives

Throughout the issue, generative AI is examined through a writing studies lens (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, p. 4) that prioritizes:

  • Rhetorical understanding over technological determinism (Li, 2025, p. 19) (Krueger, 2025)
  • Student agency over efficiency metrics (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, p. 26)
  • Critical thinking over passive consumption (Li, 2025, p. 7-8)
  • Transparent engagement over surveillance and detection (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, p. 30) (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, p. 3)
  • Pedagogical values over institutional or corporate pressures (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, p. 2)

This critical stance positions educators as neither AI advocates nor refusers (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, p. 3), but as thoughtful professionals capable of navigating complex technological change while maintaining core commitments to student learning.

5. Evidence of Student Capability

Perhaps most significantly, multiple articles demonstrate that students can and do make ethical, thoughtful choices about AI use (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, p. 5) when:

  • Given clear frameworks and expectations (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, p. 31)
  • Provided opportunities for low-stakes experimentation (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, p. 5)
  • Invited to reflect on their choices and processes (Li, 2025, p. 13) (Ryan et al., 2025, p. 42)
  • Treated as capable ethical agents rather than potential cheaters (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, p. 30)
  • Engaged in dialogue about the purposes and values of writing (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, p. 30)

This finding challenges narratives about student engagement with AI (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, p. 35-36) and suggests that pedagogical approaches based on transparency can be effective.

Critical Tensions

While offering practical frameworks, the issue also acknowledges underlying tensions: surveillance versus transparency in detection tools (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, p. 29-30), who benefits from AI adoption (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, p. 2), data privacy concerns (Uanachain & Aouad, 2025), and the friction between institutional efficiency metrics and core pedagogical values (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, p. 2). The articles consistently prioritize student agency, critical thinking, and development over technological efficiency (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, p. 5), positioning educators as thoughtful professionals navigating complex change while maintaining commitments to student learning (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, p. 5).
