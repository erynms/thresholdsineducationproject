Analysis & Synthesis

What This Issue Advances

New Rhetorical Understandings

This special issue of Thresholds makes several significant contributions to ongoing conversations about generative AI in education. These contributions span conceptual, methodological, and practical domains:

1. Conceptual Shifts

The articles collectively reconceptualize fundamental terms in writing studies:

  • Voice: No longer understood as purely individual expression, but as potentially collaborative and distributed across human-AI partnerships
  • Agency: Reimagined as "co-agency" where students maintain authority while engaging AI tools
  • Authorship: Boundaries challenged and renegotiated, opening questions about what counts as "original" work
  • Collaboration: Extended beyond human-to-human interaction to include human-AI collaboration

These conceptual shifts don't abandon traditional values in writing instruction; rather, they complicate and enrich our understanding of these terms in light of new technological realities.

2. Methodological Contributions

The issue demonstrates the value of empirical classroom studies over speculation:

  • Multiple articles report on actual classroom implementations and student responses
  • Evidence-based findings replace anxious predictions or enthusiastic promises
  • Grounded theory methods reveal patterns in AI-generated feedback
  • Student surveys and reflections provide insight into learner perspectives

This methodological grounding makes the issue particularly valuable for practitioners seeking guidance based on actual practice rather than theoretical speculation.

3. Pedagogical Frameworks

The articles provide practical approaches adaptable to varied teaching contexts:

  • Frameworks for collaborative intelligence between humans and AI
  • Assignment designs that integrate AI while maintaining pedagogical integrity
  • Assessment approaches that evaluate both products and processes
  • Guidelines for transparent AI policies in the classroom
  • Methods for building algorithmic literacy through critical engagement

These frameworks are not prescriptive but rather offer flexible starting points that educators can adapt to their own institutional contexts and pedagogical values.

4. Critical Perspectives

Throughout the issue, generative AI is examined through a writing studies lens that prioritizes:

  • Rhetorical understanding over technological determinism
  • Student agency over efficiency metrics
  • Critical thinking over passive consumption
  • Transparent engagement over surveillance and detection
  • Pedagogical values over institutional or corporate pressures

This critical stance positions educators as neither AI advocates nor refusers, but as thoughtful professionals capable of navigating complex technological change while maintaining core commitments to student learning.

5. Evidence of Student Capability

Perhaps most significantly, multiple articles demonstrate that students can and do make ethical, thoughtful choices about AI use when:

  • Given clear frameworks and expectations
  • Provided opportunities for low-stakes experimentation
  • Invited to reflect on their choices and processes
  • Treated as capable ethical agents rather than potential cheaters
  • Engaged in dialogue about the purposes and values of writing

This finding challenges deficit narratives about student engagement with AI and suggests that pedagogical approaches based on trust and transparency can be effective.

Critical Tensions

While offering practical frameworks, the issue also acknowledges underlying tensions: surveillance versus transparency in detection tools, who benefits from AI adoption, data privacy concerns, and the friction between institutional efficiency metrics and core pedagogical values. The articles consistently prioritize student agency, critical thinking, and development over technological efficiency, positioning educators as thoughtful professionals navigating complex change while maintaining commitments to student learning.
