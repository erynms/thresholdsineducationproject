Key Theme: Transparency Over Surveillance

Building Trust in the Classroom

A consistent theme across this special issue is the importance of transparency—rather than surveillance—as the foundation for ethical AI use in educational contexts.

Open Disclosure Policies

Several articles describe classroom approaches built on mutual transparency between faculty and students:

What Transparency Looks Like

  • Faculty transparency: Instructors openly share their own use (or non-use) of AI tools, their uncertainties, and their learning process
  • Student transparency: Students are asked to disclose when and how they use AI, not for punishment but for learning
  • Process transparency: Assignments include reflection on AI use as part of the learning objectives
  • Guideline transparency: Clear, explicit policies about AI use rather than ambiguous expectations

This approach positions both faculty and students as learners navigating new technological territory together, rather than as adversaries in a game of detection and evasion.

Flexible, Evolving Guidelines vs. Rigid Prohibition

The articles in this issue consistently advocate for flexible guidelines that can evolve as faculty and students learn more about generative AI's capabilities and limitations.

Characteristics of Effective Guidelines

  • Contextual: Different assignments may have different AI policies based on learning objectives
  • Explicit: Clear about what's permitted, what's prohibited, and what requires disclosure
  • Revisable: Open to modification as understanding develops
  • Pedagogical: Focused on learning rather than policing
  • Collaborative: Developed in conversation with students when possible

This flexibility stands in contrast to blanket prohibitions or rigid policies that cannot adapt to rapidly changing technological and pedagogical contexts.

Conversation and Experimentation Encouraged

Rather than shutting down discussion about AI, the approaches described in this issue actively encourage:

  • Open conversation about the challenges and opportunities AI presents
  • Structured experimentation where students can explore AI tools in low-stakes contexts
  • Reflective practice where students articulate their decision-making about AI use
  • Peer discussion about ethical considerations and best practices
  • Ongoing dialogue as new tools and capabilities emerge

This conversational approach treats AI as a pedagogical opportunity rather than merely a threat to manage.

Moving Away from Detection Tools and Policing

A significant thread across multiple articles is skepticism about—or outright rejection of—AI detection tools as a primary response to generative AI in education.

Problems with Detection-Based Approaches

  • Accuracy issues: Detection tools produce false positives and false negatives
  • Arms race mentality: Students learn to evade rather than engage thoughtfully
  • Erosion of trust: Surveillance damages student-teacher relationships
  • Misplaced focus: Energy goes toward policing rather than pedagogy
  • Inequitable impacts: Detection tools may disproportionately flag multilingual students or those with non-standard language patterns

The alternative proposed across this issue is to focus pedagogical energy on teaching students why and how to make ethical choices about AI use, rather than trying to prevent use through surveillance.

Ethical Use Through Dialogue, Not Punishment

The transparency-over-surveillance approach assumes that ethical behavior is learned through dialogue and practice, not enforced through punishment and detection.

Key Pedagogical Moves

  • Frame AI policies in terms of learning objectives rather than rules
  • Invite students to articulate their reasoning about AI use
  • Create authentic consequences tied to learning rather than punitive consequences
  • Model ethical decision-making about technology use
  • Build communities of practice where ethical norms are collectively negotiated

Implications

The shift from surveillance to transparency represents a fundamental choice about educational values. It prioritizes trust over suspicion, learning over compliance, and dialogue over punishment. The articles in this issue demonstrate that this approach is not naive—students respond to being treated as ethical agents capable of thoughtful decision-making.

However, this approach also requires more from educators: more time for conversation, more willingness to be uncertain, more flexibility to adapt as circumstances change. The payoff is classrooms characterized by trust, critical engagement, and authentic learning rather than evasion and resentment.
