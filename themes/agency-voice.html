Key Theme: Agency, Voice, and Authorship

What Happens to "The Writer"?

Generative AI fundamentally challenges traditional notions of authorship, voice, and agency in writing. Across the articles in this special issue, researchers grapple with questions about what it means to be a writer when AI can generate text on demand.

Students' Relationship to Their Own Writing

One of the most significant findings across multiple articles in this issue is that when given opportunities to experiment with generative AI, many students report that they "prefer their own writing" to AI-generated text (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, p. 33).

This finding challenges narratives that assume students will automatically outsource their writing to AI (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, p. 31). Instead, it suggests that students value their own voice and agency—but they need opportunities to discover this through experimentation rather than prohibition (Krueger, 2025) (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, p. 5).

What Students Discover Through Experimentation

  • AI-generated text often lacks specificity and personal perspective (Li, 2025, p. 12)
  • Their own writing reflects their unique knowledge and experiences (Ryan et al., 2025, p. 45)
  • The process of writing itself is valuable for thinking and learning (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, p. 29)
  • AI can be useful for certain tasks (brainstorming, outlining) while diminishing others (developing arguments, finding their voice) (Ryan et al., 2025, pp. 46-47)

Authorship Boundaries Challenged and Renegotiated

The presence of generative AI forces a renegotiation of what authorship means. Rather than viewing this as a threat, several articles in this issue explore how students navigate these new boundaries (Ryan et al., 2025, pp. 40-41):

  • What counts as "my writing" when AI generates a draft I then revise? (Ryan et al., 2025, p. 42)
  • How much revision is required before AI-generated text becomes "mine"?
  • What role does intention play in authorship when the original text came from AI?
  • Can collaboration with AI be a legitimate form of authorship in some contexts? (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, p. 34)

These questions don't have simple answers, but asking them—and involving students in grappling with them—is itself pedagogically valuable (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, p. 4).

Co-Agency: New Models of Human-AI Collaboration

Several articles in this issue introduce the concept of co-agency—a framework for understanding writing as potentially collaborative between human and AI, rather than purely individual or purely automated (Krueger, 2025) (Ryan et al., 2025, p. 51).

What Co-Agency Looks Like in Practice

  • Students use AI for brainstorming while maintaining control over argument development (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, p. 31)
  • AI generates initial drafts that students then substantially revise and personalize (Krueger, 2025)
  • Students use AI to model different rhetorical approaches, then choose their own path (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, pp. 31-32)
  • AI serves as a writing partner for feedback and revision suggestions (Ryan et al., 2025, p. 47)

The key distinction in co-agency models is that the student maintains ultimate authority over the writing and explicitly engages with the AI's contributions rather than passively accepting them (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, pp. 34-35).

Low-Stakes Experimentation Reveals Student Values

When students are given low-stakes opportunities to experiment with AI—assignments where they can explore without high grades on the line (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, p. 5) (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, p. 36)—they reveal thoughtful consideration of (Ryan et al., 2025, pp. 48-49) (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, p. 33):

  • When AI use aligns with their learning goals and when it might undermine them
  • What kinds of writing tasks benefit from AI assistance versus personal effort
  • How to balance efficiency with the learning that comes from struggle
  • What they value in their own writing and voice

These findings suggest that students are capable of making ethical, thoughtful choices about AI use (Krueger, 2025) (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, p. 5)—but they need frameworks, guidance, and opportunities for reflection to develop these capabilities.

Implications for Pedagogy

Rather than assuming that AI will eliminate student agency and voice, the articles in this issue demonstrate that generative AI can prompt valuable conversations about what we mean by authorship (Ryan et al., 2025, p. 42), why we value certain forms of writing, and how students can maintain agency even in technological environments that seem to diminish it (Blackwell-Starnes, 2025, p. 26).

The challenge for educators is to create spaces where these conversations can happen (Watkins & Monroe, 2025, pp. 3-4) (Nelson et al., 2025)—where students can experiment, reflect, and develop their own ethical frameworks for engaging with AI tools.
